# Folklore inspiration: old powers, spirits, ghosts and monsters

> **Research / inspiration bank — not established setting canon or an implementation commitment.**  
> **Compiled:** 13 November 2026 | **Version:** 1.0 | **Topics:** FOL-01–FOL-17  
> [Research index](README.md) · [Location companion](yorkshire-dales-and-cumbria-locations.md) · [World bible](../world-bible.md)

## Purpose, provenance and proposed tone

Preserve the wider folklore discussed for Azure Mines on 13 November 2026, including the reasons each tradition might be useful, its regional origin, source links, adventure hooks and integration principles. This is a structured consolidation, not a verbatim transcript. Source links are retained from the discussion; this documentation pass is not a fresh independent audit of every historical claim or live webpage.

The proposed approach is a **northern English core**, with explicitly identified borrowing from the Scottish Borders, Cornish mining traditions and the Irish Sea. These are not all Yorkshire traditions and do not form one historically unified mythology.

Distinguish three things throughout:

1. **Attested historical worship:** inscriptions, altars and archaeological evidence, such as those for Brigantia and Coventina.
2. **Recorded folklore and literary tradition:** medieval ghost stories, later collections, local legends and modern heritage summaries. A late written account does not establish an unchanged ancient origin, and a legend is not evidence that its events happened.
3. **Azure Mines adaptation:** our proposed fictional relationships, clues, quests and mechanics. These are not claims about the original tradition and are not canon unless separately approved.

The central design proposal is to build supernatural character around **relationships and obligations**. A spring has someone who watches over it; a mine has workers who expect payment; a ruined tower remembers a crime; hospitality can matter more than a sword. The aim is a landscape people have learned to live with, not a wilderness waiting to be cleared of monsters.

## Old gods and powers of the land

<a id="fol-01"></a>
### FOL-01 — Brigantia: a regional goddess

**Origin / evidence:** Northern Britain; historically attested worship. An altar at Adel near Leeds attests Brigantia. A relief at Birrens depicts an armed, winged goddess with spear, shield and elaborate crown, using imagery associated with Roman Minerva. The evidence supplies a concrete regional divine presence, but not a complete recoverable theology.

**Sources:** [Roman Inscriptions of Britain, RIB 630: Adel dedication](https://romaninscriptionsofbritain.org/inscriptions/630); [RIB 2091: Birrens sculpture and dedication](https://romaninscriptionsofbritain.org/inscriptions/2091).

**Azure Mines possibilities:** An old protector of the hill country whose shrines predate current kingdoms, with symbols surviving on boundary stones, gates and inherited jewellery. A lord invokes her as guarantor of rightful rule; villagers understand her as protector of their valley; an older shrine recognises neither interpretation. These roles are proposed adaptations, not reconstructed historical doctrine.

**Adventure seed:** A ruler removes an ancient boundary stone, unaware that the boundary marks an agreement with something other than the neighbouring estate.

**Design value:** Political legitimacy, local identity and conflicting interpretations without immediately adding a fully specified pantheon. Borrow the relationship and imagery; do not automatically import the historical name into Erith.

<a id="fol-02"></a>
### FOL-02 — Coventina: the power within a particular spring

**Origin / evidence:** Carrawburgh beside Hadrian's Wall; historically attested local water goddess. Her shrine contained numerous altars and approximately 16,000 coins, together with other offerings. A surviving relief depicts her reclining beside flowing water.

**Source:** [English Heritage, Carrawburgh collection and Coventina's Well](https://production.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/temple-of-mithras-carrawburgh-hadrians-wall/collection/).

**Azure Mines possibilities:** A sacred spring with a specific jurisdiction, not a deity who automatically governs all water. A linked watercourse might supply a village well, disappear underground and reach a pool in the mines. Mining alters the flow or damages a longstanding agreement.

**Adventure seed:** Trace the watercourse, repair a diversion, return stolen offerings or decide how to share insufficient water between settlement and mine. Killing an angry spirit should not be the default or necessarily an effective solution.

**Design value:** One of the strongest links between surface geography, underground exploration and community obligations. Particularly compatible with karst water networks, but not a new explanation of the campaign's deeper mine mystery.

<a id="fol-03"></a>
### FOL-03 — Cocidius: an old frontier protector

**Origin / evidence:** Northern Britain, including Birdoswald and Bewcastle; historically attested worship. Cocidius attracted military followers and was associated with Mars.

**Source:** [English Heritage, the people of Birdoswald](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/birdoswald-roman-fort-hadrians-wall/history-and-stories/the-people-of-birdsowald/).

**Azure Mines possibilities:** Adapt this regional military association into a guardian of passes, watch-posts and sworn duties. Small shrines could be a carved figure in a shelter, an altar at an old fort or a stone guards touch before a watch. Emphasis on keeping one's watch, rather than victory alone, is our proposed interpretation.

**Adventure seed:** A supernatural guardian remains at an abandoned fort because nobody has formally released it from an ancient order. It is still carrying out a duty, not simply attacking everything nearby. Discovering the order's authority may matter more than defeating the guardian.

**Design value:** Oaths, institutional memory and non-hostile supernatural persistence. Pair with [LOC-25, Hardknott](yorkshire-dales-and-cumbria-locations.md#loc-25) for a distinct old-fort architectural tradition.

<a id="fol-04"></a>
### FOL-04 — Manannán: sea, protective mist and modest tribute

**Origin / evidence:** Manx and wider Irish Sea tradition, not specifically Cumbrian folklore. Manannán is a sea deity and legendary ruler who protects an island through concealing mist. A particularly useful tradition has the islanders paying tribute in rushes rather than gold.

**Source:** [Manx National Heritage, House of Manannan](https://manxnationalheritage.im/our-sites/house-of-manannan/).

**Azure Mines possibilities:** A coastal power shelters an island harbour in return for a small annual observance. The offering is inexpensive, but acknowledging the agreement matters. Wealth cannot necessarily substitute for participation.

**Adventure seed:** An island long hidden from raiders becomes visible after the custom is abandoned. Alternatively, protective mist refuses to release the party until an older promise is honoured.

**Design value:** Reciprocal protection rather than transactional magic. Strong later coastal material, compatible with an island fortress or harbour community without requiring the historical deity's name to enter canon.

## Spirits of mines, households and dangerous water

<a id="fol-05"></a>
### FOL-05 — Bluecap: the unseen miner who expects fair wages

**Origin / evidence:** Northern English mining folklore in a nineteenth-century published account. Bluecap appears as a blue flame on a coal tub and assists its movement. He expects normal wages for the work: insufficient payment is refused and excess is left behind. Preserve the account's original coal-mining context; use in dwarven metal mines would be our adaptation.

**Source:** [Popular Romances of the West of England, reproduced chapter: Pitmen's Omens and Goblins](https://sacred-texts.com/book/popular-romances-of-the-west-of-england/pitmen-s-omens-and-goblins).

**Azure Mines possibilities:** A blue light appears beyond the last working lamp. An ore wagon moves through an abandoned level. Something is still finishing its shift. A former owner stopped paying unseen workers, and restoring the arrangement might reopen haulage, move equipment or help an evacuation.

**Adventure seed:** Find out who was owed payment, what work the payment covered and whether the old arrangement still applies. The useful lesson is fair dealing, not that a larger pile of coins buys a larger supernatural buff.

**Design value:** An unusually close thematic fit for Azure Mines. Keep the figure behind the light uncertain instead of immediately presenting a conventional small monster. **First-region shortlist.**

<a id="fol-06"></a>
### FOL-06 — Knockers: warnings and unwritten mining rules

**Origin / evidence:** Cornish mining traditions, not a native Dales attribution. Recorded accounts describe supernatural miners who direct worthy workers towards ore, warn of danger and punish offences. Traditions changed with the industry; warnings of collapse became especially important in industrial workings.

**Source:** [University of Exeter, Institute of Cornish Studies: Knockers, Tommyknockers and the question of ghosts](https://www.exeter.ac.uk/research/centres/ics/research/cornish-diaspora/knockerstommyknockersandthequestionofghosts/).

**Azure Mines possibilities:** A foundation for dwarven mining customs. Some knocking is rock movement; some is another shift's signal; some comes from a level sealed generations ago. Experienced miners know which patterns require retreat, which merit an answer and which should not be answered. Specific patterns would be authored game rules, not universal traditional facts.

**Adventure seed:** Decode a warning before entering a weakened working, or distinguish a genuine warning from someone exploiting miners' beliefs.

**Design value:** Knowledge of local work practices provides practical advantages. Listening and asking questions matter alongside strength. Sound design can carry clues without turning every noise into an enemy encounter.

<a id="fol-07"></a>
### FOL-07 — Hobs and boggarts: supernatural neighbours

**Origin / evidence:** Northern English household and farm traditions, including material from the North York Moors. Helpful hobs work around homes and farms; food offerings can sustain the relationship. Spying or gifts of clothing can offend or cause departure in particular accounts. Names and behaviour overlap; some traditions use boggart for a troublesome household spirit rather than a neatly separate species.

**Source:** [Ryedale Folk Museum, the helpful hobs of the North York Moors](https://www.ryedalefolkmuseum.co.uk/the-helpful-hobs-of-the-north-york-moors/).

**Azure Mines possibilities:** An unseen helper mends harness, discourages vermin or settles animals. After a change of ownership it hides tools and spoils food because an agreement has been broken, not because its species has changed alignment.

**Adventure seed:** The new owner withholds the customary supper; alternatively, a well-meaning child has given unwanted clothes. Observation and negotiation reveal which misunderstanding matters. Reconciliation need not require combat.

**Design value:** Connects directly to food, shared equipment and settlement relationships. A recurring hob should be rarely seen but unmistakably present—and not a servant the player owns. **First-region shortlist.**

<a id="fol-08"></a>
### FOL-08 — Kelpies and water-horses: help that is too convenient

**Origin / evidence:** The kelpie is principally associated with Scottish tradition; the Dales heritage source also records water-horse stories attached to the Ure and the Strid. The familiar danger is an enticing horse carrying its rider into the water. Do not assume every regional water-horse story has identical rules.

**Source:** [Yorkshire Dales National Park, folklore of the Dales](https://www.yorkshiredales.org.uk/folklore-of-the-dales/).

**Azure Mines possibilities:** An apparently ownerless horse appears when the party is exhausted, overloaded or carrying an injured companion. Help is materially attractive because transport matters. A mule's refusal to approach, abnormal tracks, an odd reflection or a local account can provide warnings. These clues are our choices for a particular encounter, not universal folklore tests.

**Adventure seed:** Decide whether to trust the animal, investigate it, bargain or take the harder route without its help.

**Design value:** Preparation, animal handling and judgement. Avoid an arbitrary fatal trap or a test that only rewards the player's familiarity with a monster name.

## Ghosts, omens and dangerous beings

<a id="fol-09"></a>
### FOL-09 — The barguest: a presence on the road

**Origin / evidence:** Northern black-dog traditions, including the Barguest of Troller's Gill. Accounts describe a monstrous dark hound, sometimes linked to death or a fatal gaze; details vary between places and tellings. Barghest is an alternative spelling.

**Source:** [Yorkshire Dales National Park, folklore of the Dales](https://www.yorkshiredales.org.uk/folklore-of-the-dales/).

**Azure Mines possibilities:** Introduce it as an omen before an opponent. A dog appears on the ridge ahead, refuses to approach the fire and leaves tracks that stop at an old boundary. When it appears beside one traveller, does it threaten them or know something about them?

**Adventure seed:** Compare local testimony with repeated sightings. One valley's hound might be a predator, another's a guardian and another's a haunting. Those are proposed fictional variations, not a universal taxonomy.

**Design value:** A recurrent presence that makes testimony and attention useful. Pair with [LOC-06, Troller's Gill](yorkshire-dales-and-cumbria-locations.md#loc-06). No obligation to turn every sighting into combat. **First-region shortlist.**

<a id="fol-10"></a>
### FOL-10 — The ghosts of Byland: unfinished business

**Origin / evidence:** Around 1400 a monk at Byland Abbey in North Yorkshire recorded local ghost stories. Many involve dead people seeking assistance, prayers or the resolution of wrongdoing, including mundane offences. The collection also contains a dangerous bodily revenant; its dead are not interchangeable murderous spectres.

**Source:** [English Heritage, podcast extras: ghosts and ghouls](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/members-area/members-magazine/podcast-extras/ghosts-and-ghouls/).

**Azure Mines possibilities:** A ghost needs a debt repaid, a false accusation corrected, stolen property returned or remains recovered. It can be frightening without being malicious.

**Adventure seed:** A haunting in an abandoned inn repeatedly lays a place for a guest robbed and buried nearby. Fighting it achieves nothing permanent; understanding the repeated scene reveals how to resolve the injustice.

**Design value:** Distinguish a spirit seeking something, a haunting repeating something and a corpse actively harming the living. These are proposed game categories, not claimed medieval classifications. They support much more variety than a generic undead encounter. **First-region shortlist.**

<a id="fol-11"></a>
### FOL-11 — Gabriel's Hounds: something hunting above the clouds

**Origin / evidence:** William Henderson's folklore collection records Gabriel's Hounds in Durham and parts of Yorkshire: supernatural dogs heard in the air and sometimes taken to foretell death or calamity. The account also discusses natural bird calls as explanations for some reports.

**Source:** [William Henderson, Notes on the Folk-lore of the Northern Counties of England and the Borders, chapter 4](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Notes_on_the_folk-lore_of_the_northern_counties_of_England_and_the_borders/Chapter_4).

**Azure Mines possibilities:** A northern spectral hunt introduced through sound. Baying circles above a moonless camp, animals panic and a companion realises it has returned. Later evidence suggests pursuit of an oath-breaker, escaped spirit or stolen object.

**Adventure seed:** Seek shelter, remain silent, bargain or relinquish the pursued object rather than automatically fight. The quarry and escape conditions would be our invention for that encounter.

**Design value:** An event moving through the world, not a routine pack of spectral wolves waiting for initiative. Preserve uncertainty between natural sounds, local interpretation and real supernatural activity.

<a id="fol-12"></a>
### FOL-12 — Redcap: cruelty's supernatural accomplice

**Origin / evidence:** Scottish Border folklore includes the murderous Robin Redcap associated with Hermitage Castle and Lord Soulis. The defining image is a cap soaked in victims' blood.

**Source:** [Historic Environment Scotland, 13 Days of Halloween](https://blog.historicenvironment.scot/2018/10/13-days-halloween/).

**Azure Mines possibilities:** A rare creature inhabits a ruined tower associated with betrayal, imprisonment or repeated murder. Bandits think they control the ruin, while its occupant encourages their worst conduct. The idea that continuing violence sustains or reproduces it is our adaptation, not a universal rule of redcap folklore.

**Adventure seed:** Defeat the creature while also addressing what keeps the place a site of cruelty. A simple kill may not settle the broader problem.

**Design value:** A genuinely dangerous supernatural antagonist, kept rare so it remains more disturbing than a goblin wearing unusual headgear. Do not automatically place it in the existing high-moor tower or make it the cause of the established conspiracy.

## Legends capable of supporting whole adventures

<a id="fol-13"></a>
### FOL-13 — The Lambton Worm: knowledge before strength

**Origin / evidence:** North-eastern English legend. In the cited telling, the hero receives a witch's advice to wear spiked armour and fight in the river so severed pieces can be carried away. Preparation and location are central to the solution.

**Source:** [National Trust, Penshaw Monument's Lambton Worm walk](https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/north-east/penshaw-monument/penshaw-monuments-lambton-worm-walk).

**Azure Mines possibilities:** A long-bodied creature occupies a gorge or underground river. Failed attacks and local accounts reveal unusual resilience. The party investigates, modifies equipment and chooses the battlefield before a decisive confrontation.

**Adventure seed:** Consult a miller who understands the water, a craftsperson able to alter equipment and a reputed witch whose knowledge is useful but whose price creates a dilemma.

**Design value:** A major monster quest that culminates an investigation rather than presenting a large health bar. Discoverable counterplay should be specific to the fictional creature, not simply a requirement to know the real legend.

<a id="fol-14"></a>
### FOL-14 — Dunmail and the sleeping king: a crown is not merely treasure

**Origin / evidence:** Lakeland legend connects Dunmail with a crown hidden in Grisedale Tarn and eventual return. John Pagen White's nineteenth-century treatment includes supernatural guardianship of the crown. A separate Yorkshire tradition places a sleeping King Arthur beneath Richmond Castle. These are legendary or literary narratives, not verified accounts of buried rulers.

**Sources:** [John Pagen White, Lays and Legends of the English Lake Country, Project Gutenberg edition](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/48207/pg48207-images.html); [Yorkshire Dales National Park, folklore of the Dales](https://www.yorkshiredales.org.uk/folklore-of-the-dales/).

**Azure Mines possibilities:** A claimant commissions recovery of a crown from a mountain lake as apparently straightforward archaeology. It is guarded, and its owner may not be entirely dead.

**Adventure seed:** Decide not only whether the old ruler can return but whether the people now living in the region would want that return. A surviving oath may conflict with present needs.

**Design value:** Ancient history complicates current politics. Old does not automatically mean wise, rightful or benevolent. Do not assign the crown to an existing dynasty or establish Erith's ancient history without approval.

<a id="fol-15"></a>
### FOL-15 — Long Meg and her Daughters: people within the landscape

**Origin / evidence:** Cumbrian stone-circle legend describes petrified people who can be released if the stones are counted correctly. The story is distinct from archaeological evidence for the prehistoric monument.

**Source:** [Visit Lake District, Long Meg and her Daughters](https://www.visitlakedistrict.com/things-to-do/long-meg-and-her-daughters-p1213161).

**Azure Mines possibilities:** The party passes a circle several times before understanding it. The count seems to change; an old drawing includes a missing stone; a nearby farmhouse contains an oddly shaped foundation block. These details are proposed clues, not historical claims about the site.

**Adventure seed:** Restoring the circle might release people, complete a protective boundary or undo an act whose purpose has been forgotten. Investigate before treating the apparent puzzle solution as the morally correct outcome.

**Design value:** Ask whether breaking an ancient enchantment necessarily puts things right. Useful beside the landscape references [LOC-07, Norber](yorkshire-dales-and-cumbria-locations.md#loc-07), or [LOC-26, Swinside](yorkshire-dales-and-cumbria-locations.md#loc-26), without conflating their real origins.

<a id="fol-16"></a>
### FOL-16 — Thomas the Rhymer: the otherworld has claims on people

**Origin / evidence:** A Border story with medieval roots, involving a journey to Elfland with the Fairy Queen. It supplies a model of supernatural encounter distinct from dungeon exploration or direct divine revelation.

**Source:** [Historic Environment Scotland, the tale of Thomas the Rhymer](https://blog.historicenvironment.scot/2021/02/tale-thomas-rhymer/).

**Azure Mines possibilities:** An ordinary hill path leads somewhere governed by unfamiliar obligations. A musician returns unchanged after years; a traveller owes a forgotten service; a hungry party is offered a feast whose terms are not immediately apparent. These are adaptation hooks rather than a claim that every detail occurs in the cited telling.

**Adventure seed:** Discover the terms and consequences of an agreement, then decide whether to honour, renegotiate or contest it. A bargain may be sincerely kept while dangerous because its participants understand it differently.

**Design value:** Otherworldly strangeness without making every fairy malicious. Conditions must be discoverable; the player should not be punished for failing to guess an invisible rule.

<a id="fol-17"></a>
### FOL-17 — Selkies: freedom rather than conquest

**Origin / evidence:** Scottish selkie traditions describe beings moving between seal and human forms by shedding skins. A lost or stolen skin prevents return to the sea. Use on our coast is deliberate borrowing, not a claim that this is specifically a Dales tradition.

**Source:** [Dig It! Scotland, seals, selkies and stories](https://www.digitscotland.com/seals-selkies-and-stories-what-does-folklore-have-to-do-with-archaeology/).

**Azure Mines possibilities:** A seemingly valuable sealskin in a merchant's collection is the means by which someone is being kept ashore. The investigation involves coercion, theft, divided family loyalties and a fishing community's offshore neighbours.

**Adventure seed:** Recover and return the skin, allowing its owner to decide what happens next rather than assuming gratitude creates a new obligation.

**Design value:** An emotionally consequential coastal quest without requiring an evil sea monster. Withholding the skin should be treated as captivity, not romantic courtship. Preserve the affected character's agency.

## Integration principles

These preserve the design recommendations from the discussion. They remain proposed guidance rather than new engine requirements.

### Keep great gods and local powers at different scales

A character can honour an established deity, leave food for a household spirit and respect a particular spring without those acts representing three rival religions. They can be understood as faith, courtesy and caution.

A priest may consider the Old Mother of the Well an aspect of a recognised deity; another may consider her independent; villagers may simply regard her as the person who has always lived there. The game need not settle every disagreement.

The world bible already names **Lethiel** as a working life-goddess identity at Harrowford. These references do not replace her, settle the wider pantheon or establish the historical gods as literal Erith deities. Prefer adapting relationships, limits, imagery and customs over importing names wholesale.

### Make folklore useful through shared supplies and expedition planning

Food can be hospitality rather than only a rest resource. Rope can recover a body whose absence sustains a haunting. Coins can settle a worker's due. A spare blanket may matter because someone asks for warmth. Pack animals can provide warnings or complicate escape.

Avoid a predictable exchange machine in which leaving one ration always grants a buff. The stronger design has the party learn relationships and become recognised over repeated visits. These ideas extend the discussed inventory, supplies, mule, horse and wagon direction; they are not claims that those systems already exist.

The mule need not be an infallible supernatural detector. Sometimes it is simply reluctant; sometimes it notices something the party has missed. Telegraph important risks through more than one channel so a single failed check does not make the outcome arbitrary.

### Teach local rules fairly

Do not make iron, salt, prayer or one plant the universal answer to all supernatural beings. Give each significant encounter a small, internally consistent set of conditions. Players can learn through witnesses, physical evidence and partial encounters.

Stories may be incomplete or contradictory, but enough reliable information must exist for informed action. Separate what villagers believe, what is actually true in the fictional encounter and what the player has discovered. Folkloric strangeness should reward attention rather than require clairvoyance.

### Preserve uncertainty and multiple kinds of resolution

Not every haunting is real. A reputed monster can conceal a crime, a misunderstood animal or a different supernatural presence. Some stories are wrong in detail yet point towards a genuine danger.

Allow investigation, restitution, negotiation, rescue, avoidance, practical repair and combat where appropriate. Do not require every ghost to be exorcised, every local power to be worshipped or every monster to be slain. Equally, retaining ambiguity does not require making every dangerous being secretly harmless.

### Keep cultures and evidence distinct

Northern England, the Scottish Borders, Cornwall and the Irish Sea contribute different traditions. Record the actual origin even when adapting an idea to the same fictional region. Local vocabulary should not be forced into rigid species classifications when the source traditions overlap.

An altar proves a dedication, not every modern interpretation of the deity. A medieval manuscript records a story, not the factual existence of its ghost. A nineteenth-century collection records versions and collectors' choices, not necessarily unchanged prehistoric beliefs. Recheck important details and original accounts before presenting research as historical fact in player-facing material.

### Do not convert inspiration into unrequested implementation

No entry here defines a stat block, spell, quest dependency, engine feature, faction affiliation or fixed campaign revelation. Do not use these notes to override the existing goblin loyalties, captive NPCs, tower evidence, secret society or undisclosed deeper-mine story. Where a concept is approved, record its specific fictional truth and player knowledge in the world bible and the relevant design document.

## First-region shortlist and an opening-cave seed

The recommended first recurring group is deliberately small:

| Topic | Everyday part of the world | Initial use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| FOL-05 Bluecap | Mine and labour | An unexplained blue light and work still being completed. |
| FOL-07 Household hob | Farm, home and provisions | Small helpful acts, followed by a dispute over an old custom. |
| FOL-02 Spring guardian | Watercourse and settlement | Shared water, offerings and an agreement people take for granted. |
| FOL-09 Barguest | Road and boundary | A recurrent omen whose intent remains uncertain. |
| FOL-10 Byland-style restless dead | Abandoned place and memory | A frightening request or repeated scene resolved through investigation. |

These are recommendations to revisit, not five approved additions or a required release scope. Knockers are a strong complementary mining idea; the spectral hunt, worm, sleeping ruler, fairy bargains and coastal beings can remain later possibilities.

### Optional seed for the existing cave

A modest offering niche beside the water is carefully kept clear of rubbish. The goblins use it too. One knows that whatever lives in the spring was there before the goblins and before the miners.

This establishes continuity without adding another boss or deciding immediately what the power is. It does not reinstate any superseded rubbish-tip room: the detail is simply a clean place beside the existing water. Its exact placement, dialogue, offering, consequences and supernatural truth remain unapproved. The waterfall feeds a pool, with a small stream continuing down a walkable corridor; do not redesign the cave to force in this hook.

### A reusable encounter-authoring checklist

For a future approved adaptation, record the FOL reference, fictional location, local belief, actual truth, discoverable clues, obligation or taboo, relevant inventory items, possible resolutions, persistent consequences and author approval. Keep player knowledge separate from author-only truth. This checklist is an editorial aid, not a new data schema or implementation task.
